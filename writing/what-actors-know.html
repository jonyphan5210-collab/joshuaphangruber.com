What actors know that most people don't

March 2026

I spent most of my twenties in rehearsal rooms, and the thing I remember most isn't the performances. It's the strange education that happens in the hours before anything is real.

Actors spend an enormous amount of time pretending to do things they haven't yet done. They practice arguments before having them, grief before losing anyone, joy before the moment arrives. This sounds like a recipe for phoniness. In practice it teaches you something surprising: how to actually pay attention to other people.

The central skill in acting, the one nobody tells you about before you start, is listening. Not waiting for your cue. Not planning your next line. Actually listening, in real time, to what the other person is doing. Because if you're not genuinely responding to them, the audience can feel it from the back row. People are exquisitely good at detecting the difference between someone who is present and someone who is going through motions.

This turns out to be useful far outside the theater.

Most conversations I've had in offices, at conferences, in the mundane back-and-forth of professional life, have a performed quality to them. People are often waiting to speak rather than listening. You can see it in their eyes -- a slight vacancy, a readiness that's just a little too prepared. They've decided what they're going to say before you've finished saying what you're saying.

The other thing rehearsal teaches you is a different relationship with failure. When you're running a scene for the fortieth time, failure isn't the exception. It's the whole point. You try something, it doesn't land, you try something else. The goal isn't to perform the scene correctly; it's to discover what the scene actually is. You find out by being wrong about it over and over until something true shows up.

I think about this a lot in the context of work, where failure tends to be treated as a problem to manage rather than a method to use. Organizations perform competence the way bad actors perform emotion: technically correct, recognizably human, but somehow hollow. Everyone can feel it. Nobody says anything.

I don't have a tidy conclusion here. I left acting and I don't regret it. But I miss the rehearsal room more than I miss the stage -- the permission to be bad at something in front of other people, on purpose, in the service of getting somewhere true.
